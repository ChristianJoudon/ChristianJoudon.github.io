CheckInn

Before the door opens.

By the time a guest arrives, several people may already have helped prepare the stay. I’m building CheckInn to keep that work connected: the reservation, the home, and what needs to be ready before someone walks through the door.

01 · The starting point

The handoff is part of the stay.

A guest leaves in the morning and another arrives that afternoon. Between those two events, the home needs to be cleaned, checked, and ready to use. Someone may also need to follow up on a repair or confirm that arrival requirements have been met. Each person sees a different part of the same day.

My work in vacation rentals has made the coordination around a reservation familiar. Dates are important, but so are the things those dates ask people to do. A calendar can show a stay perfectly clearly while leaving the team to work out the rest somewhere else.

CheckInn brings the stay and its surrounding work into one operational record. That still leaves a design problem: the person planning the week and the person preparing a home need different views. I’m building those views together, so they can share an understanding of the stay without having to sort through everything the other person needs.

The question behind CheckInn

Can the next person see what is ready, what is still needed, and where their part of the work begins?

01

Keep the day in view.

Bring arrivals, departures, same-day turns, and items needing attention together, so the team can see where the work overlaps.

02

Make room to look closer.

Keep the calendar’s place when someone filters the view or opens a unit profile. A closer look should not require starting over.

03

Show what the status means.

Distinguish a confirmed stay, a temporary hold, an owner block, and a maintenance block with labels and icons as well as color.

02 · Following a stay

The dates set other work in motion.

I use the full stay as the frame for the product: making time available, recording a reservation, preparing the home, and getting it ready again after departure. The purpose of connecting those stages is to make the handoffs easier to see.

The question changes with the person asking.

A broad calendar helps someone plan across properties. On a phone, the same information becomes a list of units and dates that can be used without shrinking the whole desktop into a few inches. Inside CheckInn

Follow the work through the application.

From the first look at the day to the next property turn, these screens show how CheckInn keeps the team in context. All images were captured from the running application using synthetic practice data.

Start with what needs attention.

Today brings arrivals, departures, same-day turns, and readiness into one view. Each row gives the operator a direct path to the next piece of work.

Keep the stay together.

The reservation record connects dates, guest party, charges, and balance with the stay's access and service history. The essentials stay visible as the operator moves into the details.

Know what is ready before the guest arrives.

The arrival queue pairs each stay with its current readiness. Selecting a property brings its requirements and available actions into view, including the work still holding up entry.

Give every turn an owner and a next step.

The turnover board brings assignments, ready-by times, and checklist progress together. Teams can see which homes need a cleaner, which are in progress, and which require another inspection.

Make pricing decisions with the evidence in view.

Suggested prices sit beside the current rate and their review status. This practice screen also makes clear when a recommendation cannot be supported and keeps approval separate from changing a live price.

03 · What a connection can tell us

A blocked night does not tell the whole story.

A calendar feed may tell CheckInn that a night is unavailable. It may say very little about the guest, the price, or the reason. I keep that distinction in the data and the interface, so a limited connection does not acquire more meaning than the information it supplies.

Local stays

Start with a record CheckInn owns.

The development build records local reservations with temporary holds, guest details, quote evidence, and confirmation. Changes and cancellations retain their history.

Calendar feeds

Keep the source visible.

Availability-only import and export are implemented with controlled test feeds. Imported events create labeled inventory blocks; they do not invent guest or payment records.

Provider connections

Add detail when it is supported.

Richer Airbnb, Vrbo, and other channel connections remain planned work, dependent on approved access and the information each provider can supply.

04 · How it is built

The stay needs a record of its own.

A reservation should remain understandable when an outside service is unavailable. CheckInn keeps its own operational records and commits a local booking independently of HAVEN. The accounting facts and attempts to deliver them are tracked separately from the booking itself.

Interface

One record, different views

Next.js and TypeScript support the calendar, reservation flow, and daily workspaces. Shared components keep selection, status, and recovery behavior consistent across them.

Commands

Check again before changing the stay.

The API checks permissions, record versions, and inventory when a change is committed. A preview helps explain the decision, but cannot replace that final check.

Operational data

Keep the responsibilities clear.

CheckInn has its own PostgreSQL database. The planned HAVEN connection uses defined accounting events, with each system keeping its own records.

Pricing research

Leave room to question a suggestion.

The pricing prototype explores how suggested rates can be reviewed alongside their supporting inputs and reasons to abstain. The current model is a placeholder, and review interactions remain in development. It does not publish rates.

05 · Getting ready

Ready should mean something.

A home can be vacant and still have work waiting inside it. CheckInn derives arrival readiness from the requirements for that stay, including housekeeping, inspection, operational blockers, and applicable payment, agreement, and access checks. Missing information remains a reason to look closer.

Housekeeping

Cleaning is one stage of the turn.

The workflow distinguishes cleaning, inspection, rework, and completion. When an inspection is required, a separate person must record the passing result.

Maintenance

A repair belongs in the arrival picture.

Open safety work and readiness blockers affect whether a guest can be checked in. Completing one task does not clear other requirements still waiting on the home.

Freshness

An old answer may need checking.

The screens identify stale or unknown information and pause consequential actions until current records can be checked. A missing result is not treated as ready.

Recovery

A lost response should not mean starting again.

Commands retain a receipt that can be checked when a response is lost. An exact retry returns the original result, helping avoid a second action when the first already succeeded.

06 · What I’m working toward

The guest arrives. The team knows where things stand.

The experience I’m working toward is quite ordinary: someone can look at the day, understand their part in it, and get on with the work. The person preparing the home should be able to see what needs attention. The person handling the arrival should be able to tell what is ready and why.

That is what connects the calendar to the rest of CheckInn. Each reservation belongs to a home, and each home has people looking after it. The software needs to keep those relationships understandable as the day changes around them.

Where the work meets HAVEN

From the stay to its financial story

CheckInn follows the stay and the work around it. HAVEN follows the financial records and their evidence. The planned connection lets the two exchange accounting facts while keeping each system responsible for its own work.
