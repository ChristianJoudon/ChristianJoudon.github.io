01 · The reason

You should not have to know where to look.

A library posts its events on one page. A community group keeps a calendar somewhere else. A flyer makes its way through a social feed, then disappears beneath the next thing. Each announcement can be perfectly useful on its own, but finding them asks people to keep track of where everything is announced.

That is the gap I wanted MatchBook to help with. Kauaʻi already has people organizing, teaching, performing, and bringing others together. The product’s job is to make more of that work visible to someone who might be glad to find it.

Bringing the listings together is only the beginning. A long directory can become another thing to sort through, so I’m designing around the questions that help someone choose: Does this interest me? Can I get there? What would I need to know before going?

The question

What would make someone pause and think, “I’d like to go to that”?

02 · Finding a place in the week

“That looks interesting” is a beginning.

An appealing event still has to fit into someone’s life. Saving gives a person time to decide, and the agenda puts those possibilities into the week ahead.

03 · The experience

Start with whatever catches your eye.

Sometimes the date comes first. Sometimes an illustration catches your eye. I wanted both kinds of browsing to lead to the details that help a person decide.

04 · The brand

A little spark belongs here.

The name and illustrations give MatchBook room to be playful. The information still needs to be easy to read.

I liked the matchbook as a small, familiar thing associated with starting something. It gave the product a visual language of its own: a drawn wordmark, loose matches, and illustrations that make the pages feel like somewhere worth looking around.

The topic motifs do practical work too. A book, a musical instrument, or a bag of groceries can suggest what kind of event a card holds before someone reads every word. Repeating those cues across the interface helps a person keep their bearings as they move between browsing and planning.

I wanted the warmth of a community noticeboard, with enough order to find what you came for.

05 · Keeping things connected

The event should still be there when you come back.

A saved listing is a small investment of attention. Refreshing the directory should preserve that connection, even as its details change.

The directory sync updates records using stable source IDs. Existing records retain the IDs that saved events and memberships point to.

Freshness

Let old details go

For fields such as cost and registration notes, a refresh can remove information the register no longer supplies. An old price should not linger simply because it was once filled in.

Reactivity

Carry changes through

Meteor subscriptions keep views connected to the underlying data. User actions go through server methods that check inputs and permissions.

Participation

Signed-in users can create events and groups, with a poster preview as they write. Editing and administration currently remain with admins.

06 · The road to launch

A useful invitation needs information you can trust.

The working product brings discovery, saving, groups, and calendars together. The next work is about keeping that information current and making the suggestions more useful.

Directory sync built · collection workflow planned

Someone may make plans around this.

Once a listing helps someone choose how to spend an afternoon, its details matter in a different way. A missing price or an old meeting time can become a problem for the person who relied on it.

The current import brings published register data into MatchBook, carries source details where available, and supports repeatable updates. The larger collection workflow is planned around a human review step between gathering a listing and publishing it.

I want automation to take on the repeated work of finding and comparing announcements, while leaving the uncertain decisions visible. A detail the source does not provide should remain a question to resolve.

The design rule

Collecting an announcement and deciding it is ready to publish are different jobs.

  1. Find the announcement

    Collect from public community calendars and keep a reference to the original source.

  2. Keep the evidence

    Preserve what the source said, including details that are absent or unclear.

  3. Bring questions to review

    Let a person resolve possible duplicates, changes, and publication decisions.

  4. Update the listing

    Carry approved changes into the directory while keeping a traceable history.

Interest matching built · learned recommendations planned

A good suggestion can be a small surprise.

Interests make a useful starting point. They can also become a narrow one if the product keeps returning the same kind of thing. I want someone who comes looking for music to have room to notice a workshop, a volunteer morning, or a group they had not thought to search for.

The current discovery views use topic and interest rules to give relevant listings more attention. Learned recommendations are a later step. Before adding them, I want a clearer account of what each action means: saving an event may mean “maybe,” and joining a group does not tell us whether someone attended a meeting.

A more complicated model would need to earn its place by helping people find better possibilities. The goal is to make choosing easier while leaving enough room to be curious.

    Start with stated interests

    Use topics, categories, and tags to support discovery. Keep time and location available as practical filters.

  2. 02

  3. 03

The working app uses rule-based matching. These later stages describe the direction of development, not a deployed learning system.

07 · What I’m working toward

The best part happens after the screen.

Building MatchBook has brought me back to how much can sit between an invitation and a decision. A person may need the right date, a little more information, or simply a way to remember something that caught their attention.

I want the product to be considerate of those small hesitations. It should help someone find their way to what is already happening around them, with enough information to feel comfortable taking the next step.

That is the experience I’m working toward: someone finds something they would otherwise have missed, makes room for it, and has a reason to go.

Where it began

From campus to island

MatchBook grew from UHnify, a team project for discovering clubs at UH Mānoa, where I worked on the interface and real-time data. I’m developing those foundations into an independent project for the wider Kauaʻi community.
